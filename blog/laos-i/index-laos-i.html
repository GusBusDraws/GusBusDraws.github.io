Laos I: Floating and Friends

Thursday, May 15th - Uncertain Waters

Getting into Laos was way easier and way faster than getting into Thailand. I'm sure this mostly has to do with the quantity of tourists entering these places, but it was still a much more pleasant experience that immediately had me feeling good about Laos. It only takes us an hour from getting off the bus in Thailand to getting on the bus in Laos, and that includes waiting for our full group and spending a not-insignificant amount of time waiting in a truck to transport us from the Thai customs to the Laos customs. We have about an hour more on a bus before we get to the river.

The border crossing town is pretty rural, so it's hard to tell if the differences we notice can/should be extrapolated between Thailand and Laos generally, but a big difference is that we're back to driving on the right side of the road. It also appears that the buildings are set back farther from the main road with some unpaved space in between, more like how the spacing was in Cambodia. Our bus drops us off at a small cafe on the river, where we're given some sandwiches and are told we have about an hour to hang out and drink some coffee before the boat leaves. Here we chat more with the folks that we shared the van with before getting to the border: Jan from Germany, Cas, Thom, and Sam from the Netherlands, and Lisa and Yoan from France. After we drink our coffee, we all go down the bank to get on the boat. It's pretty big, 20 or 30 rows with two seats on either side that face forward, then 10 or so seats at the front that face inward, and a handful of seats at the very front of the boat in an area that seems more like it's for cargo than for people. As we're getting on, we realize it's already almost full. Kaila grabs us a couple inward facing seats at the front while I take our bags to a cargo section at the back of the boat, and the other people from our bus take the uncovered seats at the front of the boat.

There were two main concerns we had about the slow boat: 1. that the boat would be over-booked and we would be packed in and/or without seats for 8 hours (which we did read were some people's experiences), and 2. that doing the long boat trip at the beginning of the rainy season was a bad idea and we would get completely soaked (we weren't able to find any slow boat accounts from the rainy season). Now that we're on the boat and have seats, we aaren't worried about point 1, and the size of the boat and the fact that we have a roof over our head makes us feel better about point 2. Even though we have seats, at first we're still a little unsure about the inward facing seats, mostly because they're different from most of the other seats on the boat. This feeling of unease goes away quickly though because we almost immediately start talking to the two guys who we sit next to: Sam from Canada and Josh from Australia. They are taking the slow boat together after meeting briefly in northern Thailand then reconvening to do the Gibbon Experience, which is a few days of ziplines and living out of treehouses in a Laos town near the Thailand border. We end up talking to them for most of the day, and eventually it comes up that they're planning to do the Thakhek Loop, a four-day motorbike loop in central Laos that Kaila and I had been considering as a bit of a redemption for not doing the Ha Giang Loop in Vietnam. We say that we had been hoping to find some more people to do it with and they say the same...

The boat ride is actually very nice! We are mostly floating through very rural areas, sometimes with villages, but mostly we see lush hills and mountains past the river banks. It only rains two or three times, and each time is only for five or ten minutes. When this happens, the folks sitting at the front of the boat come in and sit on the floor between the inward facing seats, and it's a fun "in this together" kind of vibe. The boat is selling some beer, so I have my first Beerlao experience, which I come to learn is a staple here and a source of national pride. Beerlao is everywhere!

Six hours later, our boat arrives to Pak Beng, a small town roughly halfway between the board and Luang Prabang. We are staying at a place called The Sanctuary Hotel, and learn that Sam and Thom from the Netherlands are also staying here. We even get put into rooms right next to each other. The rooms are nice, and the balcony overlooks the river. There is an elephant sanctuary across the river, and we're told we might see elephants in the morning. There's also a pool, so after we put our stuff down, we go for a quick swim to cool off before getting some dinner in the hotel restaurant and going to bed early.

Friday, May 16 - On the boat again

Breakfast is included in the hotel, so we get up and get some fruit, eggs, crepes, and coffee. As we're finishing eating, we see an elephant coming down the hill across the river! There was a big path leading down the sandy hill from the forest, and I had just assumed it was some vehicle, but now it's clear that it's an elephant path. The hill must be too steep for the elephant to walk down, because it's sliding down on its knees! When it gets to the water, it splashes around and swims for a bit, and eventually starts going up the hill with its handler in a less steep direction. Yesterday we arrangedwith Sam and Thom and the hotel to get a ride to the boat dock at 8:15am so we could get seats a bit early before we depart at 9. Through some stroke of luck or magic of the universe, we arrive at the same time as our new friends from yesterday at the perfect time for us all to get seats together. It's a different boat than yesterday, but there are still inward facing seats at the front that we're able to take. We meet Katie from Australia and Chloe from England who also sit with us. More hanging out and chatting today and watching the beautiful scenery pass. Today we decide to play some cards on a foam cooler of a guy named Jarryd from Canada who Sam knows from a previous hostel stay. We play lots of Shithead, which is essentially playing a higher card than the person before you in a pile in the center or taking the stack, until you're out of cards. It's fun, and a good way to pass the time. Even though the boat ride today is eight hours, it goes by pretty fast.

We arrive at the slow boat dock outside Luang Prabang at 5:30pm. We exchange socials with the people we've been hanging out with the past couple days and make some group chats to continue to hang out in Luang Prabang. We get a taxi truck (other people have been calling them tuk tuks so I've started referring to them as truck tuks) into town with Cas, Sam, and Thom, then walk the last strech to our hotel/guesthouse called La Nuit De Laos. This place is a bit hard for me to categorize, so I'll try describing it instead: we walk down a residential street and find this place through a gated driveway that turns into a courtyard. On the left side is the reception desk, almost looking like a ticket booth. There are rooms straight ahead, and there's a little open-air dining area to the left with four tables, a covered roof and a water station. The path curves around the rooms in an S-shape to another building behind it, where our room is up some stairs. When we enter, it's more like an apartment then a hotel room, with a living room, kitchen, and refrigerator. Pretty nice!

Our place is right be the night market, so after we drop our stuff off, we go walk around it a bit, then get some dinner. Being on the boat all day (and probably also using so much social energy) has us feeling pretty exhausted, so after this we go back to the room and turn in for the night.

Saturday, May 17 - A proper look at town

Today we decide to wake up at sunrise to see the Morning Alms, where people give offerings to the monks of the town. We were unsure how to feel about this since it feels a bit voyeuristic to observe without having any religious/spirtual stake in the matters, but curiousity got the best of us. We walked down the street the night market was set up the previous evening until we reached a temple where the street was blocked off to traffic, partially with two big signs: one advertising prices for various offerings (there were many vendors out trying to sell us things to give to the monks, mostly sticky rice and some kind of snack bars) and the other telling people how to behave, basically not to touch the monks or take pictures directly in their faces. This seems like common sense stuff, so it makes me sad to see that it's been enough of a problem in the past to make a whole sign necessary. We saw lots of people getting off tour buses and going to the chairs, then having their guides provide them with things to offer the monks. This was strange, and we weren't sure what to make of it, but it seemed to be tourists from other Asian countries, so we wondered if it was some kind of Buddhism tourism (if that's a thing).

So people sat down with their offerings, and eventually the monks came out in a long line, with a sort of pot hanging from their shoulders and a pan to go with it. As they walked down the line of seated people, they held out their pan and people would either ball up some sticky rice with a gloved hand to put in, or they'd put some of the other snacks or money in. The monks would put the offerings from their pan to their pot, then continue down the line, but we noticed a couple surprising things happening. We noticed that in addition to the line of chairs on the sidewalks, there were also baskets with plastic bags in them every ten or so meters. As the monks walked down the line of people giving them offerings, every once in a while, they'd pause at one of these baskets, and empty some of their rice and snacks into them! I guess maybe they were just getting too much. I'm not sure what happened to the rice that went into these baskets. The other surprising thing that happened was that after the line of people giving offerings, a smaller line started forming of locals. We saw one or two adults, and a handful of kids. They each carried a basket or a plastic bag. After the monks had gone through the line of people, these people held out their hands or baskets or bags to the monks, and many of them would share their offerings with them. This seemed like a very monk-like thing for them to be doing (sharing with the needy), so I was suprised to see some of the tourists looking nearly offended when they noticed what was happening at the end of the line.

Overall, it did end up feeling a bit voyeuristic to be there, but at the same time, it was interesting to see. I think I would have felt more uncomfortable if there were rowdier/less respectful tourists that we were sharing the experience with, but it was a small crowd when we were there (maybe like ten people watching and not participating in giving alms, I hesitate to even call it a crowd). I think the fact that it's the off season for tourism and also this happening at sunrise probably deters many people.

After watching the morning alms, we go back to bed for a few more hours. When we get back up, we check out the breakfast provided at the guesthouse. I get an omelet that comes with some sausages and a baguette and Kaila gets some fruit. The coffee is instant, but that's made up for by also giving us a choice of another drink to have with out breakfast, so I get a mango-pineapple smoothie. Some of the folks from the slow boat are going to a zipline park today, and we decide to join, and we have a few hours before we meet up with them. Originally we were going to go to the National Museum, but they are closed for lunch for pretty much the entire window we have open. We get some coffee at a place called Indigo Cafe, then decide to go to the Traditional Arts and Ethnology Center instead. This is a lot like the ethnology museum we went to in Vietnam that explained about the different ethnic groups that make up the population of Laos, some of which are the same as Vietnam (which makes sense since the countries are neighbors and many of these groups moved south from China. There was also a super interesting special exhibit that showcased some of the research work of the Center first by explaining an incident where a fashion company plagiarized some designs from a local ethnic group. The group was not legally empowered to do anything about it since current copyright law is not sufficient for protecting cultural traditions and crafts. The exhibit then pivoted to describe the Center's role in the development a system that would use the "three C's rule" (credit, consent, and compensation) to both protect the traditional crafts and give the groups the power to collaborate with outside groups if they so desire. The center set up a model that would be able to respond to cases of cultural appropriation such as the incident with the fashion company, which included setting up a database to document traditional designs. The work is being carried out in an open way, such that the tools can be used by other groups that want to do the same. The project is really cool and I encourage you to read more about it!

After the special exhibit, we checked out the gift shop and met one of the researchers and senior staff at the museum, Ton, who told us some more about the museum and mentioned he leads tours to villages in the surronding area where you can see some of the traditional crafts. We ask if he has availability tomorrow, and he says he could take us to see Kuang Si Waterfall, then go to three different villages afterwards. We agree, and he says he'll pick us up from our hotel at 8:30am.

After the museum, we meet up with Sam, Thom, Cas, Jan, Lisa, and Yoan for ziplining. This has kind of turned into a hail mary attempt because the reservation system on the website is down, and none of our attempts to reach the company have been successful we decide we'll just show up and see if they will let us do the course. The only problem with this plan is that the park is about a half hour out of town. We find a truck tuk driver willing to take all of us out there and wait in his truck while we're in the park, and go.

The ride out there is actually very pretty and has us feeling better about the possibility of the ziplines not working out, but when we arrive, we are welcomed in. The course in the park has six "jungle bridges" and seven ziplines, and ends up being really cool. We get some great views of the jungle, and it feels safe the whole time, with us always having two lines from our harness to a steel safety cables at all times. When we got to the end of the course, we walked back to the start area on some paths on the jungle floor. It was nice to pair our views from above with some views from below, too. It started raining on our way back, so we definitely got soggy.

When we get back to town from ziplining and change into less soggy shoes, Kaila and I go over to the hostel that Canadian Sam and Aussies Josh and Katie are staying at so we can get dinner with them. Some other folks from their hostel join: Shash and Sasha from Australia, Alaina from the US. We go to a place called Bamboo Garden which has a big open air dining room. The folks there are really nice and let us push a bunch of tables together to accomodate our big group (which will get bigger because some of the ziplining folks plan to come meet us too). I get a minced fish salad which sounds weird but is tasty, it's basically rice with flakes of cooked white fish and a bunch of fresh herbs.

After dinner, we walk around the town looking for a place to get some drinks. We find a bar called E'Strange that has a nice patio out front that is raised up a set of stairs from the streer. We decide to sit in a couple tables on one half of the patio that we can push together. Chloe from the slow boat comes to join also, and we hang out here for an hour or two. At some point while we are here, a woman comes and sits at the table on the other half of the patio. She is behaving erratically, taking what looks like travel books and magazines in and out of a bag, and also opening and closing an umbrella. She's wearing big sunglasses, and occasionally goes down to the street to check on a motorbike. At one point she crosses the street to go behind some bushes, and comes back with a different outfit and a large stick (that she luckily leaves at the motorbike). It's not clear if she is on drugs or having some kind of a mental health episode, but I think many of us try to be aware of the situtation for safety purposes without being too nosy about what is going on. We can see the owners of the bar also monitoring the situation, so it doesn't feel unsafe.

After a couple drinks, we decide to move on. Apparently all the bars in Luang Prabang close at 11pm, and the only place that's open later is a bowling alley at the edge of town where many of the travelers end up at the end of the night. We decide to check it out, but first go to a conveinence store to get some drinks because the ride there is long. On our way, we run into Jarryd from the slow boat, and he joins us. We get our truck tuk, and head out to the bowling alley.

A surpring thing about the bowling alley is that it also has an archery range out front! Before we go in, we each shoot a few arrows. We end up chatting with the attendant there for a bit, and learn that he lives next door and decided to get a job here at night since the bowling alley is loud and keeps him awake anyway. I guess making the best of an annoying situation. I'm not so good at shooting arrow, but when we go inside to bowl, I win the first game I play! We have friends on three lanes, so for the second round of games, the winners from the first round decide to play each other. I don't win this one, but I also don't get last place which feels like a half win. A surprising thing happens when another team tries to get the same lane they played on before. The folks running the alley tell us someone has already paid for a game on that lane, and when we look back, we realize it's the woman with the umbrella from the first bar, and she even still has the umbrella (and is still opening and closing it). Kind of concerning to think about how she got there, but it felt too strange not to share here.

After the second round of games, we go back to the city center. Some of our friends negotiated the truck tuk driver to make a stop at the only 7-Eleven in town to get some late night snacks. I think I've already talked about the famous 7-Eleven toasties that get warmed up at the counter in a special panini press, but another famous item here among travelers are the lava cakes, which are mini chocolate cakes that also get heated up. I haven't tried one yet, so this seems like a good opporunity. I get a ham carbonara toastie which sound weird but was actually very good and a lava cake that is also tasty. A good late night snack for sure that gives us just the right amount of energy to get back to walk back to our room from the city center and get to sleep right around 1am.

Sunday, May 18

Today we are doing the waterfall and village tour with Ton from the Traditional Arts and Ethnology Center. We get up for the provided guesthouse breakfast and he picks us up at 8:30am. His car is a silver sedan that is pretty ordinary besides a sick wolf decal on the hood. Major kudos for that. We're going to the waterfall first, which is about an hour and a half drive. It's nice to have this time to learn more about the Center, Laos, and the people that live here while we enjoy the view of the countryside.

We get to the parking of the waterfall then take an electric cart the rest of the way in to the start of the trail where there are also some shops and vendors. After we start walking, we quickly come to some sloth bear enclosures, and learn that the park that has the waterfall also runs a small conservation center for the sloth bears that are endangered because of poaching. Apparently one of the reasons they keep them in captivity is to protect them from poachers. I've seen these kind of bears in zoos in the US before, but it's cooler to see them in an environment that's closer to where they live naturally. They are pretty goofy looking animals.

We continue the hike which has been more of a walk so far and eventually get to the waterfall, which is actually one big waterfall and series of smaller cascading falls between big clear, blue pools that are sort of scalloped on the edges, almost like the pool formations that we've been seeing in caves. I guess it makes sense that there are similar formations here, because the waterfall is falling from and onto limestone.

There's a metal staircase off to the side of the waterfall that supposedly goes to the top. Ton says we can take this up and come back down another path across the water from us. We hike up and after about 20 minutes, we're at the top. But there's more! There's a cafe built into a treehouse here, so for another ~1 USD, you can go up to the canopy and get a view over the trees. The treehouse itself is pretty interesting, so the price seems worth it just to see that, but we also want to see the view around!

After a few minute at the top of the treehouse, we come back down and cross a couple plank bridges across the water above the waterfall, then take a steep and slippery path back to the bottom of the waterfall to meet back up with Ton, and walk with him back to his car.

The next part of our tour today is the villages! We are going to three. The first is a Khmu village. The Khmu people are know to be expert weavers. We meet a woman here that teachs us about the practice of spinning cotton into string, then how that cotton is used for weaving. We also see a rice whiskey operation on a farm, similar to the rice wine (happy water) we had in north Vietnam. This is pretty crazy to see! There are a bunch of jugs of fermenting rice, then a big boiler that's doing the distilling. Since it's a farm, there are also a bunch of animals here. Some fresh piglets, chicks, and ducklings, and all their parent animals, too.

The next village has a mix of people of different ethnic groups. We park under a huge tree, then walk over to the house of a rice farmer and blacksmith. He shows us his furnace and a chunk of metal from an old bomb that he uses as an anvil. He makes agriculture tools from scrap metal, showing us some knives he made from old scrapped truck brakes. He also makes his own animal traps from bamboo and rope. He shows us a few different rat and bird snare-type traps that work by pulling a piece of rope into tension using a piece of bamboo as a "spring". Really cool, and when the traps aren't in use, they fold up along the piece of bamboo for easy storage.

The final village we see is a H'mong village. There are H'mong people in Laos who have come from China like the H'mong people in north Vietnam. Here Ton passes the tour off to a local who shows us through a small museum where we see demonstrations of traditional crafts and tasks including weaving, grinding corn, and husking rice. The H'mong man also shows us a bamboo wind instrument that sounds a lot like a bagpipe. He plays for us, then gives us a demo of a traditional dance that is paired with the playing of the instrument at special celebrations. It's really impressive to see the talent associated with playing the instrument while dancing around!

The H'mong village was the last part of the tour, so after this, Ton drives us back to our hotel. We're pretty hungy by now, so we go into town to get some food at a restaurant called Bouang. Kaila gets a crispy rice salad dish called Nem Khao that comes with some lettuce and betel leaves for making wraps. I get Lao meatballs that come with fried betel leaves. Both dishes are very tasty! After food, we go back to the room for a bit. We decide we want to hike up to a temple in the middle of town called Phousi Hill. We want to see the sunset there, so we head up early to get a good view. There aren't many people at the top when we get there, so we're able to get a spot where we can hang our legs over the side wall. Eventually more people arrive, including some of the folks from the slow boat, and the space fills us.

When we get back down, it's dinner time, and Kaila and I decide to go back to Boaung so I can try the Nem Khao and she can get some more. Afterwards, we hear the Dutch people from the slow boat are going to a bar to watch a Netherlands football match because a couple of them are supporting opposing teams. This sounds like a fun thing to be a satellite for, so we decide to stop by. When we get there, we realize how serious one of the folks is taking the match when we see that he brought a tablet to the bar to make sure they could watch. Luckily his team is the one that wins, and it turns out its for the championship so I guess it's a big deal! Some of the other slow boat folks come by too, and it turns into a fun little reunion of the folks we were playing cards with on the boat. I play some pool with Canadian Sam, and only win by default when he accidentally sinks the cue ball while going for the eight ball. After a couple drinks at the bar, we head back to the room and turn in.

Monday, May 19 - Heading to Nong Khiaow

Today we get up early to pack because we're taking a bus to a town in the north called Nong Khiaow! This town has some big mountains directly surrounding the town and is known for some great viewpoint hikes. A lot of people do an overnight trip from Luang Prabang where they hike up and camp on one of the peaks, but we decide to take a bus in so we can take some more time in the town. We are going with Candian Sam and AUstralian Josh from the slow boat. Kaila and I eat some quick breakfast, then a truck tuk picks us up at 10:20am to head to the bus station.
