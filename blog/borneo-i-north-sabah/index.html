Sat Jun 7 - Heading to Sepilok, Sabah (East Malaysian Borneo)

Today we get up and pack our bags to fly to Borneo! Borneo is a big island that is split between three nations: Malaysia, Brunei, and Indonesia. We are going to be spending some time on the Malaysian side, and today we're landing in a city called Sandakan. Our airport time mostly went smoothly, but there was a slight hiccup when we dropped off our bags. We purchased two 14 kg bags, but our first bag was a bit over, which meant that weight was subtracted from the second bag without alerting us first. There weren't any people running the bag drop (it was totally automated) and there weren't any useful warnings messages. I just kept getting an error for "technical reasons" then finally found a human-ran check in where I learned the problem. Damn you robot capitalists! ANyway, after that was all figured out, we still had a bit of time to grab some food from an airport lounge Kaila got access to with her credit card before going to our gate. The gate was strange, because we had to show our boarding passes to even be let into the seating area, but then we didn't have to again before getting onto the plane. I've never seen that before! The flight was about three hours, which gave me some blog and reading time.

Sun Jun 8 - Rainforest Discover Center

Mon Jun 9 - Starting our Uncle Tan Kinabatangan River tour

Today we're starting our tour of the Kinabatangan River, which has some wildlife corridors connecting conservation areas protected from deforestating for palm plantations. This is a good place to see wildlife, but the unfortunate reason for this is because there isn't much jungle left as an alternative in this area due to the extent of development. More on this later.

We decided to book a three day, two night tour through a company called Uncle Tan, which ended up being a great choice. The tour starts in Sepilok, then brings us to a village where we'll take a boat up river to the company's base camp where we'll be staying. We're getting picked up from the Orang Utan Conservatory in Sepilok, which is pretty close to our hotel. The hotel gets us a ride to the Conservatory, stopping first at a market so we can get some cash and pasta sauce for GF pasta Kaila is bringing in case she can't eat the food at the camp. The timing is close, but we get to the center only a few minutes late and meet the five other people we'll be doing the tour with: Lewis from England, Voutter from the Netherlands, and Davide, Franco, and Luca from Italy. We squeeze into a van with all our bags for an uncomfortable hour and a half drive, but then we get to the village and spend the same amount of time on the boat, but it's much more comfortable. One of the guides, Eim, drives us out to the camp, pointing out some wildlife on the way. The highlights were a young female saltwater crocodile and an oriental pied hornbill (full list of all the animals we saw later in the post).

When we get to the camp, we see that the floating dock seems to be in a place that it's not supposed to be, clear because it's no longer floating. Eim tells us this is from a flood that happened two weeks ago. We scramble up the sideways dock and the steep and muddy river bank toa trail that leads into the camp. Eventually we get to a boardwalk that connects the shelters of the camp. There's a big shelter in the middle of camp. It's probably about 200 square meters, lifted about a meter off the ground with a roof and railings, but otherwise open to the elements. This is where we'll eat our meals and spend most of our down time when we're not on tours. There's a couch area, a big picnic table with five or ten others, a small bar area, connected to the rest of camp with boardwalks.

We meet Lan here, who is the manager of the camp and the head guide. He will be our guide for the next few days! He apologizes for the state of the camp (even though it really doesn't seem that bad besides the dock). Lan explains that the most recent flood was the sixth of that size so far this year while in previous years they would only expect one flood of that size for the whole year. He then shows us where the boardwalks go: one to a smaller hut to the side which can float during floods, another to the camp kitchen (also a floating hut), and the third connects to the path to the boat dock and also branches off to the shelters and the camp toilets.

Lan shows us to the shelters where we'll be sleeping. They're also open to the air, but have mattresses and bug nets. The other boys are staying in the first shelter, which sleeps five. The next shelter is occupied by four people that got here the day before. Kaila and I are staying at the next one, and we get it all to ourselves!

After our shelter, there are a few more on the way back to the hill with the rest of the camp toilets and the "jungle shower". The camp toilets are basically outhouses, but have ceramic toilets with a drum of water pumped in from the river and a bucket for flushing. The jungle shower is a platform with a couple buckets next to some more drums of pumped river water. Luxury!

After we get settled in, we go back to the main hut for a briefing from Lan about living in the jungle camp and our itinerary for the next few days. He also warns us about the monkeys that sometimes often try disrupt the camp, and are referred to as the Jungle Mafia.

After the briefing, we eat some dinner. The camp cook has made us steamed rice, chicken, eggplant, pumpkin, and some tiny bananas for desert. By the time we finish eating, it's nearly time for our first tour: a night safari on the boat. We change into some long sleeves and get our headlamps for navigating the path to the boat dock, then we set off. It's cool to see the river at night! We travel upstream, towards a conservation area up an estuary that feeds the Kinabatangan. Lan scans the river banks and the trees while he drives the boat, which is impressive multi-tasking. It's also interesting to see his strategy for looking for wildlife, discernible from the way he moves his flashlight. His spotting skills are top-notch, making his 25+ years of guiding believable. Some of the highlights from the night are a civet we were able to follow for a few minutes as it patroled the river bank, a baby crocodile that let us park the boat directly next to it for observing, an owl perched in a dead tree only a few meters above the water, and some sleeping proboscis monkeys. We stay out for about an hour and a half, then head back to camp and turn in for the night.

Tue Jun 10 - A full day in the jungle

Today we start our day early, waking up at 5:30am so we can get some coffee before our morning safari. We head over to the boat at 6, and this time we make it all the way to the conservation area. We see a lot of wildlife! Lan was determined to help us see a wild orang utan, so he kept us out for two and a half hours and managed to see one! So amazing to see them in the wild. We saw a young male hanging from a tree, Lan guessed maybe around eight years old since he was small, but seemed to be on his own. We watched him for about twenty minutes, hanging in a tree close to the river bank and only about ten or 15 meters above us. I'm very grateful for this spotting! We saw some other great wildlife too, with some of the highlights including a rhinoceros hornbill and a few very brightly colored stork-billed kingfishers.

When we get back to the camp, the cook has breakfast ready for us. We have noodles, fried rice, fish and cabbage, and banana fritters. After breakfast, we have about an hour before our next tour, which is a walk through the jungle. Lan tells us he'll be pointing out plants of interest, including some that can be eaten and used for medicine, and we begin. He tells us about bamboo, the local fig trees that are an important food source for many of the local animals. At one point he shows us a keutom tree, which he explains is a traditional medicine in the local villages. The leafs can be boiled and consumed as a remedy for a range of ailments. The tree is now a point of controversy on the mainland however, as it has become over-used Politicians don't agree on whether to classify it as a drug or not. It's at this point Kaila makes the connection that this medicine is what we know as kratom, which has similar disagreements in the States as it's sold at many smoke shops. I have heard of a few people getting pretty stuck on the stuff. Interesting to see it growing in the wild!

We continue our walk and although Lan had told us not to expect to see much wildlife, we still manage to spot quite a few interesting critters! Some of the highlights are a really cool leaf mantis that is camoflouged against a tree branch, a frilled tree frog, a tiny lesser narrow-mouth frog, a Malaysian giant forest hunstman spider wrapped around a tree trunk, some wild pheasants, and a really cool lantern fly.

When we get back to camp, we eat some lunch consisting of rice, chicken, cabbage with sardines, and cucumbers with chili. We spend some chill time reading and relaxing in some hammocks set up in the trees outside the main shelter, then Lan asks if some of us would be willing to help get the boat dock into better shape after the damage from the big flood. By now we are feeling so grateful of the things that Lan has showed us that we are happy to help.

We start the work of getting the dock into better shape by bringing some long 12 foot beams and planks from near the main shelter over to the bank by the dock. When we get there, we see that Eim has already done the hard of pushing the floating platform back into the water all by himself! Impressive, because it's probably 12 square meters with a bunch of plastic drums underneath for buoyancy. However, this isn't all the work that needs to be done, because the other platforms, stairs, and bridges that were used to get down the steep bank to the floating platform have either washed away or are buried by mud. The next step to make the dock more easily navigable is to dig some stairs into the side of the bank, then build a bridge that can span from lower down on the bank onto the floating platform.

Lan gets his chainsaw, then we set up the two long beams and I hold the planks in place while Lan cuts them, with Lewis, Davide, and Franco following behind with nails and hammers to nail the planks in place. Once we have the bridge done, we dig stairs into the bank, making one estra large so we can fit a remaining, smaller bridge into the bank to hold the larger bridge we just built. Then we carry the bridge down, and finally help replace some rotten planks on the floating platform before calling the work complete. It's clear that Lan is very talented with the chainsaw, and he tells us he has milled all of these planks free hand, using only the chainsaw! Extremely impressive because the planks are all really straight and consistent. He says most of the logs he uses float into the camp during the floods, so that's the one good thing that comes out of them.

After we finish repairing the dock, we go back to the main hut and Lan buys us some beers from the camp bar. He tells us he's grateful for our help, and in return, he will giv eus more time on our evening safari tonight. We take turns using the jungle shower, then at 4:30pm we head out, using the repaired dock for the first time!

There are a few highlights of this safari. The first is all the probsocis monkeys we see! In the evening, they come to the trees closer to the river so if they feel threatened in the night (by big snakes, for example), they can jump into the river to escape. We got to see many families hanging out by the river, and got some great views of the big males that lead these groups with their big noses and pot bellies. The second highlight were sightings of two more hornbill species: the bushy crested hornbill and the Asian black hornbill. This brings our sightings to five of the eight species in Borneo. The final highlight, perhaps the highest, was sunset on the boat as all the flying foxes took to the skies to go out and search for fruit. We started counting them, but stopped after we reach 103. We think we probably saw 300 or 400 flying foxes! So cool to see these huge mammals all across the sky. Once it was dark, we started to head back to the camp.

When we got back to camp, we hung out with the cats and played some Uno until it was time for a dinner that was similar to last night, but also had a fish dish tonight.

Wed Jun 11 - Extra jungle time

Today we wake up early again for another morning boat safari, which will be our last tour. Today Lan is taking us down river (instead of up which is the direction we went for the other tours) because he knows a section of river where some gibbons like to hang out, and we haven't seen any yet. The tour starts strong with a sighting of a monster saltwater crocodile on the river bank! Lan spotted it from probably 500 meters out, which is impressive. Today one pair of binoculars damaged in the flood was dry enough to bring, so we were able to get some good views of the croc. He tells us it's probably 4.5 meters long! We were able to get pretty close before it swam off, which was also cool to see.

Thu Jun 12 - Bus to Semporna, Sabah

Fri Jun 13 - Snorkeling

Today we're going snorkeling on a multi-island trip we booked through BorneoTravelNetwork.com. We get up early to pack a daypack and go to a restaurant in front of the hotel where I get some chicken fried rice and Kaila gets an omelet. A van picks us up from our hotel, then drives us over to the boat jetty where we get on a boat operated by a company called Dive Monsters. The first stop is on an island called Timba Timba, which has a small forested area with a couple small houses and a long sandbar on either side. The tour guides tell us the stop is to take photos on the beach, which is kind of annoying but much of the group seems pretty into it. After some time here, we get back on the boat and go to the otherside of the island for snorkeling. The reef is really interesting! The coral is alive, which is not something we've seen before snorkeling in the Guld of Mexico. We see a lot of tropical fish, including some big angel fish.

The next stop is Mataking island, a bigger island with a larger forested area that has an area with many picnic tables where the companies bring their groups to eat lunch. Our group gets lunches from metal trays packed by the Green Cafe, which is funny because this restaurant was right next door to the restaurant we ate breakfast at, but wasn't open yet, probably because they were busy making these lunches! The meal consists of rice, veggies, a slice of omelet, and some fried chicken. We have some free time on island after lunch, which we spend walking on the side of the island opposite the boats, which has a beautiful beach with coarse sand. There are lots of little crabs here, including some tiny hermit crabs!

After lunch, we go to a snorkel spot on the other side of the island and see even more living coral! The coral here is even more colorful and varied, with tons of different shapes: trees-like, round, big plates (which when you look closely can also see has a similar branching form that has grown into a flat plane), and even some brain coral. We also see some anenomes, one that has a couple clown fish living in it! There's even blue tangs here, which add to the Finding Nemo vibes.

The last island we go to is Pandanan Island. The snorkel spot here is right on the edge of a steep drop-off into much deeper ocean, which means there are some really interesting views of the variety of coral and tropical life with a deep blue background. It feels a bit silly, but it made me feel like I was in a screensaver from the early 2000's. Kaila had a much more elegant description of feeling like we are in a nature documentary. There is some truth to this actually, because much of David Attenborough's documentary Blue Planet 2 was filmed off the coast of Sidapan Island, just a short boat ride from here! Another interesting part of this reef is feeling many different water currents. As we swam around, we would enter some areas where the water felt hot or very cold, and it would change quickly without much of a gradient. Kaila said she thought she could see the currents, and looking that up later, apparently that can be possible.

After this last bit of snorkeling, we get back in the boat, wait for some scuba divers to rejoin who split off earlier, then we head back to Semporna. Once we're there, Kaila and I decide to walk over to the place on the map marked as the Tawau Bus Station right by a KFC in town to see if we can buy bus tickets to Tawau for tomorrow. Unfortunately, there isn't anybody there. I remember the tour guide telling us that there may be buses to Tawau by one of the Shell gas stations, and see that there's something else marked as the Tawau Van Terminal nearby, so we walk over there. We find a man on the curb by some vans and he tells us there aren't tickets for the vans, people just show up and the van leaves when it's full. He says we should show up an hour or so before we want to leave. Now that we know this, we start walking the 15 minutes back to our hotel, stopping at TwenTea again on the way. This time I got a cheesy matcha with boba that had a savory cheese foam on the top that had a taste almost like cottage cheese, but a bit sweeter and with a totally smooth texture (i.e. no chunks). After we get back to the room and shower, Kaila realizes she got a reply back from the 1StopBorneo folks, and they have an opening for us to do a three day, two night tour at their accomodations at the Tawau Hills Park. We weren't planning on doing another tour, but a few days in the primary rainforest of Tawau Hills Park is too good to pass up, and we think we can fit it into the rest of our budget for Malaysia. We book the tour, and head back to Summer Breeze Cafe so Kaila can get some more of the lamb that didn't make her sick. I get a fried chicken sandwich, and it's so good! Crispy on the outside, but not dry on the inside, with lots of flavor and a spicy sauce. One of the best chicken sandwiches I think I've had, after a couple restaurants in Washington, DC. After dinner, we walk back to the room, watch the season finale of Top Chef that just came out, and go to bed.
